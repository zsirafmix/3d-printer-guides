Safety first

A diode laser can injure eyes and skin, start a fire, and produce fumes. This is not optional equipment advice. Official Laser Engraving Protective Goggles on the LONGER store are specified for 190–550 nm, optical density OD 6+, about 40% transmittance, with typical wavelengths listed as 405, 445, 515, and 532 nm, and compatibility copy that also lists 450, 473, and 520 nm. Confirm those figures on the live page against the wavelength of the machine you actually buy. Wear them. Do not look into the beam or a reflection.

Work in a ventilated area, keep a fire-safe surface and a way to put a fire out, and do not leave a job unattended. The official Ray7S description is the only one in this comparison that calls out Class 1 laser safety and a fully enclosed design. The Ray5 mini, Ray7, and B1 pages I used do not make that Class 1 / fully enclosed claim, so treat them as open-frame machines that need eye protection and ventilation. The Ray5 mini official description does list built-in X/Y limit switches and an emergency stop. That is useful, not a substitute for goggles or supervision. I am not publishing a laser-class number I did not see on a product page.

Pros and cons, honestly

I have not timed these machines or cut a test grid for this article. The advantages below are official page claims or direct price/size facts. The drawbacks are either also official (air pump not included, small work area) or ordinary limits of an open-frame diode laser.

Ray5 mini — pros. Lowest official US price in this group, pre-assembled, small footprint, and a clearly published 130 × 140 mm work area. Official safety hardware includes limit switches and an emergency stop.

Ray5 mini — cons. That work area is small. Official marketing says 300+ materials, including metal; I am not treating that as a promise that a 2.5W head will cut thick stock, and I did not test it. It is not described as a Class 1 enclosed machine, so goggles and ventilation still matter. Rotary sets on the same page cost substantially more than the Basic kit.

Ray7 — pros. Official speed of up to 36,000 mm/min, a stiffer-sounding dual-shaft X-axis, and power steps from 7.5W to 40W on one product page, starting at $229.99 for 7.5W.

Ray7 — cons. It is $140.00 above the Ray5 mini 2.5W Basic at today’s official prices, and the official description says the air pump is included only on the 40W model. Autofocus and camera are optional. The page I used does not claim Class 1 enclosure.

Ray7S — pros. This is the official enclosed, Class 1 option, with Klipper vibration compensation and the same 36,000 mm/min speed claim. That is the cleanest official safety story in this lineup.

Ray7S — cons. The 7.5W Basic is $50.00 above the open Ray7 7.5W today, and the gap stays large at higher powers (the 20W Basic is $529.99 vs $429.99 for Ray7 20W). Air assist is still optional below 40W. I have not compared cut quality.

B1 — pros. Official work area of 450 × 440 mm is in a different class from the Ray5 mini. Interchangeable 20W / 30W / 40W / 2W IR modules and named software support (including LightBurn and LaserGRBL) are the reason to consider it. The 20W Basic at $479.99 is a large official markdown from $799.99.

B1 — cons. It costs several times the Ray5 mini, and I did not see a Class 1 enclosed claim on the B1 description I used. Higher official speeds (up to 600 mm/s) are a manufacturer figure, not a test I ran. Pro, IR, and air-related bundles on the same page raise the total quickly; I am quoting the Basic Set as the entry price.

Which one should a beginner buy?

If you want to try engraving small pieces for the least official outlay, the Ray5 mini 2.5W Basic at $89.99, plus goggles at $8.99 if you need them, is the starting point I would use. Step to the Ray7 7.5W at $229.99 when the official speed and power range matter more than the lowest price. Choose the Ray7S when you will actually use an enclosure and want the official Class 1 design. Choose the B1 only if the official 450 × 440 mm area or IR/module path is required; it is not a first-dollar beginner buy.

FAQ

Do I still need goggles with the Ray7S?

The official Ray7S page calls out Class 1 safety and a fully enclosed design. Follow the live manual for that model, and do not assume an enclosure lets you skip every precaution, especially if a door is open, a window is damaged, or you are aligning anything with the power on. For the Ray5 mini, Ray7, and B1, plan on rated eye protection.

Is the 40W Ray7 a better beginner machine than the 7.5W?

Not automatically. It is officially $669.99 (was $899.99) and is the Ray7 power that includes an air pump. A beginner who only needed the 7.5W price would be overspending unless they already know they need that power.
